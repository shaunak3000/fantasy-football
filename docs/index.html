The Rice Ball Draft Rule

Six more sophisticated draft strategies were benchmarked against the real 2025 draft. The simplest one won, and the most elaborate one lost by 241 points.

The entire strategy

Take the highest‑ranked player at a position you still need.

That is the whole thing. It is executable from memory if every piece of software fails, which is the point — the tooling exists to make this rule fast and to keep you honest about roster limits, not to replace it.

01 — The evidenceEverything cleverer lost

Each strategy replayed the real 2025 draft at all eight slots, fit only on prior seasons, then scored by what its players actually went on to do.

The result was not close, and it was not the one the design predicted. Ordering by consensus rank and refusing to exceed a roster cap beat every attempt to be smarter about it. The Monte Carlo lookahead board — the most engineered thing in the repository — finished 241 points behind at 5.2 standard errors.

The rollout confirmed the diagnosis rather than rescuing it. Judging a pick by the finished roster instead of the roster so far moved the same machinery from −241 to −10, at hundreds of times the cost, to produce the same picks as the one‑line rule.

The uncomfortable finding

The projection pipeline contributes nothing to the draft. Both the winner (1793) and pure best‑available (1422) order by raw consensus rank — the entire 371‑point gap between them is the roster‑cap filter, not any model. And best VOR at need losing to best available at need says it from the other side: converting consensus rank into our own value estimate destroys information, because expert consensus already aggregates injury news, depth charts and holdouts that a mechanical rank‑to‑points curve cannot recover.

02 — Where the points areThe draft is the small half

Measured on the same 2025 replay, using only projections that existed at the time.

This is why the draft rule is allowed to stay boring. Sixteen picks made with entirely public information, against a consensus that already encodes it, is the worst available place to look for an edge. In‑season there is real asymmetry: your specific roster, your specific weekly matchup, and opponents who set lineups carelessly.

One notable null result belongs here too. A matchup‑aware objective — solving for exact win probability against that week’s opponent rather than maximising points — picked essentially the same lineup as plain projection‑maximising: +2 points over a whole season, with only two of eight teams ever diverging. With a full roster, variance gaps between startable players are small next to mean gaps. The 200 points come from starting the right players, not from risk cleverness.

03 — FormatWhy this league’s shape drives everything

Three properties of an eight-team full-PPR league that are easy to get wrong.

Eight teams compresses positional scarcity. Only 72 roster spots start each week, so replacement level is very high and the waiver wire stays deep all season — a streamed quarterback is worth about what QB8 is worth. The scarcity gradient that powers a normal value‑over‑replacement board is much flatter here. A board that recommends an early quarterback in this league is miscalibrated, not clever.

Half the league makes the playoffs, and matchups are one week long. P(first) ≈ P(top 4) × P(win semi) × P(win final), where qualifying is the cheap term and the title comes down to two single‑game outcomes. Seeding is worth little beyond making it. So the regular season is played for floor, and weeks 14–15 are played for ceiling.

Waiver priority, not FAAB. The transactions problem is sequential — when to spend a priority position — not a budget allocation.

04 — ByesOne bye week matters; the rest do not

Measured by drafting a roster at all eight slots, then re-solving the lineup with each bye week’s players removed.

Regular‑season bye collisions are cheap. On a 110‑point lineup they cost 1–5%, and bench depth absorbs them — one slot had five players on bye in week 11 and lost only 9.9 points. With four of eight teams making the playoffs, a single soft week rarely decides qualification.

Week 14 is the exception, because week 14 is the semifinal. Two teams are on bye then.

What this does not justify

Avoiding week‑14 players outright. You have to reach the semifinal before a week‑14 bye can cost anything, and the top of the board is where qualification comes from. The free version of this fix is narrower: take a kicker whose team is not on bye in week 14. The entire kicker position is worth 1.4 points a week over replacement, so K1 against K4 is noise — and it removes the single largest bye exposure at no cost.

05 — Draft nightThe runbook

The draft slot is randomised one hour before kickoff, so the order of these steps is load-bearing.

  1. 5:00 PMRefresh the ESPN cookies. A sudden 401 means they expired; the tool now names the file it is actually reading.
  2. 5:05 PMlive_draft prepareExpect a scrape date of 09‑04. Seeing 08‑28 is normal, not an error — the consensus feed publishes weekly on Fridays.
  3. 5:10 PMlive_draft planThe shape of all eight possible draws.
  4. 7:30 PMRoom opens. The slot is drawn now.
  5. 7:31 PMlive_draft plan <slot>
  6. 7:35 PMlive_draft watchMust start after the shuffle — it detects your slot at startup, so an earlier start locks in the wrong one.
  7. 8:30 PMDraft in ESPN as normal. No typing; the board follows the live feed.

If the feed dies, manual recovers the picks already made from ESPN and lets you continue by typing. If everything breaks, the strategy is the one sentence at the top of this page.

06 — HonestyWhat is measured, and what is not

Kept separate on purpose. Confidence here is earned per component, not granted to the project as a whole.

Measured against real outcomes

The scoring engine reproduces 1,922 of 1,922 player‑weeks in 2025 and 2,165 of 2,165 in 2024, exactly — a hard gate everything downstream depends on. Projection intervals are calibrated: nominal 50/80/90% intervals cover 52.3/80.5/88.7% of held‑out outcomes under leave‑one‑season‑out over 2021–2025. The lineup optimizer’s +200 and the draft rule’s +81 are both replays scored on what actually happened.

Advisory — never validated

Waiver and trade evaluation is priced in title probability but has never been scored, and is blocked rather than skipped: ESPN’s activity endpoint returns HTTP 400 for 2024 and 2025, so there is no add/drop history to test against. D/ST projections are fitted on two seasons only. Rookie uncertainty is not modelled at all — a rookie ranked 40th is priced exactly like a veteran ranked 40th, though his outcome distribution is genuinely wider.

Untestable with the data that exists

Average draft position is the one behavioural signal available — not another opinion about who is good, but a measurement of when the room actually takes people, restamped daily against a consensus board that is a weekly scrape. The gaps are large: one receiver sits at consensus 72 and goes at ADP 123.

It appears on the board as a column and a “can wait” note, and it cannot change a pick. ESPN publishes only a live snapshot, and no historical preseason ADP exists in the data, so the benchmark that killed six strategies has nothing to replay for a seventh. Untestable signals stay advisory.

The metric gap

The strategy benchmark scores rosters by total season points, which is structurally blind to when points arrive. A week‑14 bye is invisible to it. So any bye‑aware selection rule is currently untestable, and building a playoff‑weighted roster metric has to come before building the rule.
